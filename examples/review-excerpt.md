## Themes

The agent grouped the 51 papers into six thematic sections rather than
summarising them one by one. The themes are questions and findings, not method
categories:

1. **The presentation bottleneck and the mechanisms that enforce it** — why
   antigen expression and antigen display are different quantities.
2. **Releasing hidden antigen reservoirs** — interventions that surface peptides
   the tumour was not presenting.
3. **Re-engineering the processing and presentation machinery** — direct
   manipulation of the pathway itself.
4. **Conventional and targeted therapies as presentation modulators** — the
   presentation side-effects of treatments given for other reasons.
5. **From more presentation to better T-cell recognition** — whether increased
   display actually translates into recognition.
6. **Gaps and future directions**, then **Conclusion**.

Theme 5 is the load-bearing one: it is where the review tests whether the
interventions in themes 2–4 deliver the recognition benefit they are meant to,
rather than assuming more display is self-evidently better.

## Opening (first ~300 words of the review)

> ## Introduction
>
> Neoantigen-directed immunotherapy rests on an assumption that is easy to state
> and hard to satisfy: that a tumour-specific protein alteration will be
> processed into a peptide, loaded onto a human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecule,
> and displayed at the cell surface in enough copies, for long enough, to be seen
> by a T cell. Each step in that chain is an opportunity for the tumour to
> intervene. Antigen processing and presentation machinery (APM) defects are now
> recognised as determinants of response and resistance to immune checkpoint
> inhibitors rather than incidental features of advanced disease [1,2], and the
> integrity of the pathway has been proposed as a definition of cancer immune
> responsiveness in its own right [3].
>
> The practical consequence is that antigen expression and antigen display are
> different quantities. A systematic test of this gap overexpressed 125
> combinations of full-length neoantigen and a single HLA class I allele and used
> immunoaffinity purification with tandem mass spectrometry to ask which
> predicted neoepitopes were actually presented; presentation was observed in
> only 22% of predicted cases [4]. The same study found that the reported
> association between HLA loss of heterozygosity and predicted neoepitopes was
> not supported for 58% of the combinations tested, and that among 51 sequences
> used in personalised mRNA neoepitope vaccines, clinical responses were
> independent of whether the encoded neoepitope could be shown to be presented. A
> review of paediatric solid tumours reaches the same conclusion from the clinical
> direction, proposing that such tumours may be "antigen-invisible rather than
> antigen-poor" and that antigen expression does not reliably predict natural
> presentation [5].
>
> If display rather than mutation is frequently the limiting step, then
> presentation becomes a therapeutic target. This review asks two linked
> questions of the recent literature: by what means can neoantigen presentation
> be increased, and where interventions do increase it, does T-cell recognition
> measurably improve? The emphasis throughout is on studies with peptide-level
> evidence — immunopeptidomics by mass spectrometry — rather than inferred
> antigen load, because the central claim under examination is precisely about
> what reaches the cell surface.

## Why this excerpt is representative

Three properties of the agent's output are visible in these paragraphs:

- **Synthesis over summary.** The opening draws on five papers to make one
  argument, rather than describing five papers in turn.
- **Quantities attached to their source.** "22% of predicted cases", "58% of the
  combinations", "51 sequences" each sit against the citation they came from.
- **A stated scope limit.** The last sentence commits the review to
  peptide-level evidence and says why, so a reader knows what was excluded by
  design rather than by oversight.
